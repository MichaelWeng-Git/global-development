This is my reflection for our Humanity unit on global development. The unit asked one question: why are some countries rich and some poor? In class we watched Metro Manila. We ran a village simulation. And we used Gapminder, a website that sorts every country into 4 income levels.

The big idea I took away is that every society has layers. Some people are on top. Some are on the bottom. And once you're born into a layer, it's really hard to move out. Teachers call this social stratification.

Three things mostly keep a family in their layer. Money: what your family already has. People: who your family knows. And knowledge: the stuff you grow up knowing, like how to talk to a teacher. Most poor families don't have any of the three.

But Gapminder also shows the world is slowly getting better. Most people now live in the middle, not at the extremes. Extreme poverty has dropped from about 50% in 1980 to around 9% today.

To show what I learned, I'm going to zoom in — from this whole planet down to one room in one house. My three lessons sit at the bottom of the zoom.

More than half of the world's poor live on this one continent. So if you actually want to look at poverty, this is where it is. Asia is also where the biggest drop in poverty has happened recently — like over the last 30 years, hundreds of millions of people in China and India moved out of extreme poverty. But the gap inside Asia is still really big. The Philippines is kind of in the middle of that gap.

In the Philippines, about 1 in 6 people live below the poverty line — but that one number kind of hides a lot. There are 7,000 islands and 117 million people, all really different. The country's biggest export is actually its workers — more Filipino nurses, domestic helpers, and seafarers work abroad than at home, and a lot of families just live off the money sent back from relatives. Manila is where most of the wealth is. It's also where most of the poverty is — often only a few streets apart.

13 million people live on one delta about the size of London. Tall glass buildings and cardboard shacks kind of share the same skyline — sometimes literally the same street. The inequality isn't hidden here, you can see it from a plane. The Pasig River runs through the middle of the city, and right north of it on the west side is Tondo — about 630,000 people squeezed into 9 km², which is around 4 times the density of the rest of Manila.

01.Lesson 01 · hierarchy
So one of the main things I took away is that the world can't really have zero poverty. I don't think it's actually possible. Every country in history has had a hierarchy — there's always someone on top and someone at the bottom. That's just how humans have always organized themselves. Even after revolutions and reforms, the hierarchy kind of comes back. So I don't think "everyone being equal" was ever really the deal. The question isn't really whether we can fix poverty completely. It's more like — how big do we let the gap get, and how much do we care about the people at the bottom?

02.Lesson 02 · the cycle
The other thing I learned is that once a family is poor, it's really hard for them to get out. They don't have savings. They don't really know anyone who can help them get a better job. They don't have time or money for school. So if you just do what everyone around you is doing, you'll end up exactly where they are. The only way out is doing something different. That's what hit me in Metro Manila — the family in the movie doesn't escape by working harder. They escape by taking a risk no one around them would touch. And it almost destroys them.

03.Lesson 03 · luck + choice
And the last thing I learned was that sometimes one lucky thing can really change someone's life. Meeting the right person, getting a random job, even having a special season. I noticed it in the village simulation, and I see it in real life too.

But luck alone doesn't really do much. Two people can get the exact same chance, and one takes it and the other doesn't. Even the people who get lucky can still make the bad choice. So that's why the decision also matters.
